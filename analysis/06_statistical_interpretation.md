# 06 — Statistical Interpretation

## Purpose

This section explains how selected effect estimates from the IQWiG A25-111 assessment were interpreted.

The focus is on:

- Relative risk (RR)
- Confidence intervals
- The null value
- Statistical significance
- Precision
- Clinical interpretation

---

## 1. Relative Risk

Relative risk (RR) compares the probability of an event occurring in the intervention group with the probability of the event occurring in the comparator group.

Conceptually:

RR = Risk in intervention group / Risk in comparator group

For outcomes where a lower event rate is favourable:

- RR < 1 suggests fewer events with the intervention
- RR = 1 suggests no difference
- RR > 1 suggests more events with the intervention

The direction of interpretation depends on whether the outcome represents a favourable event or an adverse event.

---

## 2. The Null Value

For a relative risk, the null value is:

**RR = 1**

An RR of 1 means that the estimated event risk is the same in both groups.

For example:

**RR 0.63**

suggests a lower event risk in the lecanemab group.

However, the point estimate alone is not sufficient to determine whether there is statistically significant evidence of a difference.

---

## 3. Confidence Intervals

A confidence interval describes the uncertainty around an estimated treatment effect.

For example:

**RR 0.63 [0.35–1.14]**

The point estimate is 0.63.

The confidence interval extends from 0.35 to 1.14.

Because the interval includes the null value of 1, the result does not demonstrate a statistically significant difference.

The estimate should therefore not be interpreted as definitive evidence of a treatment benefit.

---

## 4. Example: CDR-SB

For mild Alzheimer's dementia with AChEI therapy:

**RR 0.80 [0.53–1.20]**

Interpretation:

- The point estimate is below 1.
- This is directionally consistent with fewer deterioration events in the lecanemab group.
- However, the confidence interval includes 1.
- Therefore, the result does not demonstrate a statistically significant difference.

The appropriate interpretation is therefore:

> The analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in CDR-SB deterioration between the treatment groups.

It would be inappropriate to state simply:

> "Lecanemab reduced CDR-SB deterioration by 20%."

The latter statement ignores the uncertainty represented by the confidence interval.

---

## 5. Example: ADAS-Cog14

For mild Alzheimer's dementia with AChEI therapy:

**RR 0.63 [0.35–1.14]**

The point estimate suggests a potentially lower risk of deterioration with lecanemab.

However:

**0.35 → 1.14**

includes 1.

Therefore, the result does not establish a statistically significant difference.

The wide interval also indicates uncertainty around the estimated effect.

---

## 6. Example: EQ-5D VAS

For mild Alzheimer's dementia with AChEI therapy:

**RR 0.66 [0.37–1.16]**

Again, the point estimate is below 1, but the confidence interval crosses 1.

Therefore, the evidence does not demonstrate a statistically significant difference.

This illustrates why the point estimate should never be interpreted in isolation.

---

## 7. Example: QOL-AD

For mild Alzheimer's dementia with AChEI therapy:

**RR 0.56 [0.27–1.14]**

The point estimate suggests fewer deterioration events in the lecanemab group.

However, the confidence interval includes 1.

Therefore, the analysis does not establish a statistically significant difference.

The relatively broad interval also indicates uncertainty regarding the magnitude and even the direction of the underlying effect.

---

## 8. Safety Outcomes

The interpretation changes depending on the outcome.

For an adverse event, an RR above 1 generally indicates that the event occurred more frequently in the intervention group.

For example, infusion-related reactions were more frequent with lecanemab.

This demonstrates why treatment effects must always be interpreted in relation to the outcome being measured.

An RR greater than 1 can represent:

- harm, if the outcome is an adverse event
- benefit, if the outcome is a favourable event

Therefore, the numerical value cannot be interpreted without knowing the outcome definition.

---

## 9. Statistical Significance vs Clinical Relevance

Statistical significance and clinical relevance are not the same concept.

A statistically significant result does not automatically mean that an effect is clinically important.

Conversely, a clinically meaningful effect may fail to reach statistical significance if the estimate is imprecise.

An HTA assessment therefore requires consideration of:

- magnitude of effect
- confidence interval
- patient relevance
- methodological quality
- consistency
- uncertainty

---

## 10. Why Confidence Intervals Matter in HTA

Consider:

**RR 0.63 [0.35–1.14]**

The point estimate alone might appear favourable.

However, the confidence interval indicates that the true effect could plausibly range from a substantial reduction in risk to a value compatible with increased risk.

This uncertainty is important when deciding whether the evidence is sufficient to establish an added benefit.

---

## 11. Evidence Interpretation in This Case

The relevant ZVT-conforming analyses generally produced effect estimates below 1 for several efficacy and quality-of-life outcomes.

However, the corresponding confidence intervals crossed 1.

Therefore, these analyses did not establish statistically significant advantages for lecanemab.

At the same time, safety outcomes demonstrated increased treatment-related adverse events, particularly infusion-related reactions.

The evidence therefore needs to be interpreted as a combination of:

**Potential efficacy signal**

+

**Statistical uncertainty**

+

**Methodological uncertainty**

+

**Observed safety burden**

rather than as a simple positive or negative clinical trial result.

---

## 12. Key Statistical Lesson

The most important lesson from this analysis is:

> A point estimate is not a conclusion.

A robust interpretation requires the effect estimate, confidence interval, outcome definition, comparator, population and methodological quality to be considered together.

This is particularly important in HTA because the objective is not merely to determine whether a treatment has a numerical effect.

The objective is to determine whether the available evidence is sufficiently relevant and reliable to support a conclusion about patient-relevant added benefit.
