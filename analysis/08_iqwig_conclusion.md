# IQWiG Final Conclusion

## Overall Assessment

The IQWiG assessment A25-111 evaluated the added benefit of lecanemab compared with the appropriate comparator therapies for adults with early Alzheimer's disease.

The assessment considered three research questions:

1. MCI due to Alzheimer's disease versus watchful waiting.
2. Mild Alzheimer's dementia with lecanemab plus an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor (AChEI) versus AChEI therapy.
3. Mild Alzheimer's dementia with lecanemab monotherapy versus AChEI therapy.

## IQWiG Conclusions by Research Question

| Research question                      | Comparator       | Evidence conclusion                                                   | Added benefit  |
| -------------------------------------- | ---------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------- |
| MCI due to Alzheimer's disease         | Watchful waiting | Relevant evidence did not establish an advantage of lecanemab         | **Not proven** |
| Mild Alzheimer's dementia + AChEI      | AChEI therapy    | Relevant evidence did not establish an advantage of lecanemab + AChEI | **Not proven** |
| Mild Alzheimer's dementia, monotherapy | AChEI therapy    | No relevant comparative study was available                           | **Not proven** |

## 1. MCI Due to Alzheimer's Disease

For patients with MCI due to Alzheimer's disease, the appropriate comparator therapy was **watchful waiting**.

The relevant analysis did not provide sufficient evidence to establish an added benefit of lecanemab compared with this comparator.

Several efficacy outcomes showed numerical estimates that could appear favourable for lecanemab, but the confidence intervals were wide and crossed the null value.

For example:

* CDR-SB deterioration: RR 0.88 [0.51–1.52]
* ADAS-Cog14 deterioration: RR 0.86 [0.39–1.89]
* EQ-5D VAS deterioration: RR 1.02 [0.67–1.54]
* QOL-AD deterioration: RR 1.00 [0.56–1.78]

These results do not establish a statistically clear advantage.

The overall conclusion was therefore that **added benefit was not proven**.

## 2. Mild Alzheimer's Dementia + AChEI

For patients with mild Alzheimer's dementia receiving an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor, the appropriate comparator was continued AChEI therapy.

The relevant analysis compared:

**Lecanemab + AChEI**

against

**Placebo + AChEI**

Examples of efficacy estimates included:

* CDR-SB deterioration: RR 0.80 [0.53–1.20]
* ADAS-Cog14 deterioration: RR 0.63 [0.35–1.14]
* EQ-5D VAS deterioration: RR 0.66 [0.37–1.16]
* QOL-AD deterioration: RR 0.56 [0.27–1.14]

Although several point estimates were below 1, the confidence intervals included the null value.

Therefore, the evidence did not establish a statistically clear advantage for the relevant population and comparator.

IQWiG consequently concluded that **added benefit was not proven**.

## 3. Mild Alzheimer's Dementia — Lecanemab Monotherapy

For mild Alzheimer's dementia, the appropriate comparator therapy was treatment with an acetylcholinesterase inhibitor.

However, IQWiG did not identify a relevant comparative study evaluating:

**Lecanemab monotherapy versus AChEI therapy**

This is an important distinction.

The absence of a relevant study does **not** mean that lecanemab monotherapy was demonstrated to be ineffective.

Instead, there was insufficient comparative evidence to establish an added benefit under the requirements of the assessment.

The appropriate interpretation is therefore:

**Added benefit could not be established because relevant comparative evidence was unavailable.**

## Benefit–Harm Perspective

The benefit assessment also needs to be considered together with safety.

Infusion-related reactions occurred more frequently with lecanemab in the relevant evidence.

For example, the IQWiG assessment reported substantially more infusion-related reactions in the lecanemab groups than in the comparator groups.

Symptomatic ARIA was also an important safety outcome requiring careful interpretation because the number of events was relatively small and the resulting estimates were imprecise.

This illustrates an important HTA principle:

**Evidence of possible benefit must be considered together with potential harms and the certainty of both estimates.**

## Why the Overall Conclusion Matters

The case demonstrates why an HTA assessment is more than a simple comparison of treatment-group averages.

The overall conclusion depended on several linked questions:

1. Was the correct patient population analysed?
2. Was the appropriate comparator therapy used?
3. Was the evidence relevant to the German healthcare context?
4. Were patient-relevant outcomes assessed?
5. How large were the observed effects?
6. How precise were the estimates?
7. What methodological limitations affected confidence in the evidence?
8. Were potential benefits balanced against safety concerns?

The answer to these questions determines how much confidence can be placed in an apparent treatment effect.

## Key HTA Lesson

A treatment can produce favourable numerical estimates in a randomized clinical trial without automatically establishing an added benefit in a German HTA assessment.

The evidence must be:

**relevant → comparator-aligned → methodologically credible → sufficiently precise → clinically meaningful**

before it can provide strong support for an added-benefit conclusion.
