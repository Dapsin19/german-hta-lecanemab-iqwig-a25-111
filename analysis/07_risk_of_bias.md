# Risk of Bias Assessment

## Purpose

Risk of bias describes the extent to which the design, conduct, analysis, or reporting of a study may systematically distort the estimated treatment effect.

For this case study, the focus is on the evidence considered relevant by IQWiG for the German early benefit assessment of lecanemab.

## Overall Assessment

IQWiG assessed the risk of bias for the relevant analyses as **high**.

This is important because an observed difference between treatment groups cannot automatically be interpreted as a reliable estimate of the treatment effect if methodological limitations may have influenced the result.

## Key Sources of Bias

### 1. Treatment Modifications

Patients' treatment situations could change during the study.

Treatment modifications can complicate interpretation because the treatment actually received over time may differ from the originally assigned treatment strategy.

This is particularly relevant in long-term trials of progressive diseases such as Alzheimer's disease.

### 2. Disease Progression

Disease progression can influence treatment exposure, discontinuation, and subsequent observations.

When patients progress differently during a trial, later outcome measurements may no longer represent a straightforward comparison of the originally assigned treatment groups.

### 3. Treatment Discontinuation

Discontinuation of study treatment can introduce bias when the reasons for discontinuation are related to treatment efficacy, adverse events, or disease progression.

If substantial numbers of patients discontinue treatment, the final observed outcomes may no longer represent the full randomized population in the same way.

### 4. Missing or Potentially Informative Observations

Missing outcome data can become a source of bias when missingness is related to treatment response, disease progression, or adverse events.

Therefore, the interpretation of the available observations requires consideration of how patients who did not provide later measurements may differ from those who remained in the study.

## Why This Matters for HTA

A statistically estimated treatment effect is only as informative as the evidence supporting it.

In an HTA context, the assessment therefore considers not only:

* the size of an effect estimate;
* confidence intervals;
* statistical significance;

but also:

* whether the comparator represents the required standard of care;
* whether the relevant patient population has been analysed;
* whether outcome measurement is appropriate;
* whether treatment modifications may affect interpretation;
* whether treatment discontinuation introduces bias;
* and whether missing data could affect the conclusions.

This is one reason why an HTA assessment cannot be reduced to simply asking whether a clinical trial produced a statistically significant result.

## Application to A25-111

The CLARITY AD trial provides randomized evidence for lecanemab, but IQWiG's assessment of the relevant analyses identified methodological limitations that resulted in a high risk-of-bias assessment.

The issue is particularly important because the German benefit assessment did not simply use the overall trial population. The relevant analyses had to reflect the populations and comparator strategies corresponding to the German research questions and appropriate comparator therapy.

Consequently, even when numerical treatment effects appeared favourable in some analyses, they had to be interpreted together with the methodological limitations and uncertainty surrounding the relevant evidence.

## Key Lesson

**Risk of bias does not mean that a study is useless.**

Instead, it means that greater caution is required when using its results to support a causal conclusion.

For Market Access and HTA work, the practical lesson is:

> Evidence must be assessed for both its numerical results and its methodological credibility.

A strong HTA analysis therefore connects **study design → risk of bias → uncertainty → confidence in the estimated treatment effect → overall benefit assessment**.
